Acquisition is the easy part. Handover is where RTM companies lose money, usually by accepting
whatever figure the outgoing party offers for accrued service charges and by inheriting
contracts nobody has read.

## What has to be handed over

**Accrued uncommitted service charges.** The landlord must pay over any service charge money
held that has not been committed to expenditure. Either side can ask the First-tier Tribunal to
determine the amount if you cannot agree it, so an unsatisfactory offer is not the end of the
conversation.

**Contract information.** On request, the landlord must supply the information the company
needs about contracts relating to the management of the building.

**Uncommitted** is the word that does the work. Expect the outgoing party to treat a large part
of the balance as committed. Ask, in writing, for the basis of that: what was ordered, when, by
whom, under what contract, and whether the order predates the claim notice.

## Scrutinising the accruals

Before agreeing any figure, ask for:

- The service charge accounts for the last three years, plus the current year to date.
- Bank statements for the service charge account and any reserve fund account.
- A schedule of arrears by flat, with ages.
- The reserve fund balance and what the lease says it can be spent on.
- A list of every open purchase order and every commitment said to be uncommitted-no-longer.
- Copies of all invoices above a threshold you set £500 is a reasonable starting point.

Look specifically for management fees charged beyond the acquisition date, insurance commissions
retained by the agent, works ordered in the window between the claim notice and acquisition, and
any inter-company balances.

If the numbers do not reconcile, get an accountant to look before you sign a settlement. A
one-off fee is small against a reserve fund shortfall you inherit permanently.

## Contracts and contractors

You take over the building with its existing arrangements. Go through each one and record: what
it covers, what it costs, the notice period, whether it was entered into after the claim notice,
and whether it was consulted on under section 20 where required.

Long-term agreements of more than twelve months are the ones to watch cleaning, lifts, door
entry, communal heating. If you intend to replace them, check the notice periods before the
acquisition date rather than after.

## Data you must ask for

You will need, at minimum: the full leaseholder contact list, the health and safety file and
fire risk assessment, asbestos register where applicable, lift and other statutory inspection
records, insurance policy and claims history, utility account details and meter readings, plans
and drawings, warranties and guarantees, and any correspondence about known defects or disputes.

Once you hold leaseholder data you are a data controller under UK GDPR. Set up the basics —
a privacy notice, a retention policy, and access limited to those who need it at handover
rather than a year later.

## Doing it yourself

None of this needs a solicitor. The work is administrative, and a determined treasurer with a
spreadsheet will do it better than an agent who is billing by the hour. Where it becomes worth
getting help is a contested accruals figure, a large reserve fund, or a building with fire
safety remediation in progress.
