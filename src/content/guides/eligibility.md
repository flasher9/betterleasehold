The Right to Manage is a no-fault right. You do not have to show that the freeholder or the
managing agent has done anything wrong, and you do not need anyone's permission. What you do
have to show is that the building, the leases and the level of participation all meet the
conditions in Part 2 of the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002. Get one of them wrong
and the claim can be defeated on paper.

There are four questions to answer about the building, and then a shorter list of situations
that take a block outside the scheme altogether.

## Is it a self-contained building, or a self-contained part of one?

A self-contained building is one that is structurally detached. That covers most purpose-built
blocks standing on their own.

A self-contained *part* of a building is harder, and this is where claims most often run into
trouble. The part must be divided vertically from the rest of the building, it must be capable
of being redeveloped independently, and the services serving it such as: water, drainage, electricity,
gas must either already be independent, or be capable of being made independent without
significant interruption to the rest of the building.

The practical consequence is that horizontal splits do not work. Flats on the upper floors of a
building with a shop underneath cannot be carved out on their own; the claim has to be made for
the whole structure, which then brings the non-residential limit into play.

Where an estate has several blocks, each block is normally its own set of premises and needs its
own claim and, in practice, its own decision about whether to use one RTM company or several.

## Are enough of the flats let on long leases?

The building must contain at least two flats held by qualifying tenants, and the flats held by
qualifying tenants must be at least two-thirds of the total number of flats.

A qualifying tenant is someone holding under a long lease broadly, a lease originally granted
for a term of more than 21 years. It does not matter how much of the term is left, so a lease
with four years to run still counts. It does not matter whether the leaseholder lives there;
buy-to-let landlords qualify. Assured shorthold tenants, company lets on short terms and
business tenancies do not count.

Two things people get wrong here. First, the test looks at the lease as originally granted, not
at the residue. Second, a flat can only have one qualifying tenant at a time, so a sub-let does
not create a second vote.

If part of the building is in non-residential use shops, offices, a surgery, a gym the
internal floor area of those parts must not exceed 50% of the internal floor area of the whole
building. Common parts are left out of the calculation on both sides.

This limit was 25% until 3 March 2025, when section 49 of the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act
2024 came into force. A large number of mixed-use blocks were deliberately built with commercial
space above 25% but below 50% precisely to defeat RTM claims. Those blocks are now in scope.

Floor area is the one part of the eligibility test where it is usually worth paying somebody. If
the split is anywhere near the line, a measured survey from a surveyor working to a recognised
measurement standard is far cheaper than losing a tribunal application over it.

## Will enough leaseholders take part?

At the date the claim notice is given, the RTM company's membership must include qualifying
tenants of at least half the flats in the building. Not half the participating flats half of
all the flatsssss.

In a block of 20 flats you need 10 members. In a block of two flats you need both. Membership
means having actually been entered in the company's register of members, so get the paperwork
done before the notice goes out rather than relying on people who have said yes verbally.

## When the Right to Manage is not available

Some buildings are outside the scheme however well they score on the tests above:

- The landlord is a local housing authority.
- The building is a converted house containing four flats or fewer, and the freeholder, or an
  adult member of their family has lived in one of the flats as their only or principal home
  for the last twelve months. This is the resident-landlord exception and it defeats a
  surprising number of small conversions.
- An RTM company is already in place for the building, or the right was acquired and then
  ceased within the last four years.

## What to gather before going further

Before spending anything, collect the title register and title plan for the building from HM
Land Registry, one sample lease, the number of flats and which are let on long leases, and the
names and addresses of every leaseholder. You will need all of it for the notices, and the
exercise itself usually surfaces the problems early a missing intermediate landlord, an
unregistered title, a block that turns out to be two buildings.

If the building looks like it qualifies, the next step is forming the company.
